I've reviewed hundreds of design portfolios as a hiring manager in New York. The honest truth: most get about two minutes. The good news is that two minutes is plenty, if you structure your work for the way people actually read it.

## Lead with three case studies, not twelve

Quantity signals uncertainty. Pick your three strongest projects and go deep. If a hiring manager wants more, they'll ask in the interview, and that's exactly the conversation you want.

## Use a simple, repeatable structure

Every case study should answer the same questions in the same order:

1. **The problem.** What was broken, for whom, and why did it matter to the business?
2. **Your role.** What did _you_ do, and who did you work with?
3. **The process.** Two or three key decisions, with the evidence behind them.
4. **The outcome.** What changed? Numbers are great; honest qualitative results are fine too.

<Callout title="The two-minute test">
  Ask a friend to skim one case study for two minutes, then explain the problem and your impact back to you.
  If they can't, tighten the top of the page.
</Callout>

## Show decisions, not just deliverables

Polished final screens are expected. What sets you apart is showing the fork in the road: the option you didn't pick and why. That's what senior designers look for, because it's what the job actually is.

## Write like a human

Skip the jargon. "We increased checkout completion by 12% by removing a step" beats "leveraged a user-centric paradigm to optimize conversion funnels." Clear writing is a design skill, and reviewers notice.

## Make it fast and accessible

A portfolio that takes eight seconds to load, or can't be read on a phone, has already lost. Compress your images, use real text instead of text baked into images, and check color contrast. Your portfolio is itself a design artifact.

> Your portfolio isn't a gallery. It's an argument that you can solve the next company's problems.
